truth metaphysics horwich

In summary, Horwich holds that a true proposition should follow the equivalence schema below:

┌p┐ is true⟺p

There might be some part I forgot about the quotation mark ┌┐. Highlighted parts, section 5, and the last section were written by me, while the rest was reorganized Horwich’s handout.

INTRODUCTION

This document is divided into 5 sections.

In section 1, I’ll indicate how deflationary accounts of truth differ from traditional proposals about the nature of truth — and why a deflationary account is preferable.

In section 2, I’ll begin my assessment of which is the better deflationary account of the meaning of “true” — as between, on the one hand,

(i) My use-theoretic, so-called minimalist suggestion, whose heart is the view that the meaning of the English word “true”, when applied to (or denied of) a proposition, consists in the inclination of English speakers to accept instances of the schema: “The proposition that p is true ↔ p” — which is known as the Equivalence Schema.

And on the other hand,

(ii) Wolfgang Künne’s ingenious, but controversial, definition of “x is true” as

(∃p)((x=p)∧p)

which departs from classical logic by invoking quantification into sentence positions.

It’s worth noting that my above qualification of “the meaning of the English word ‘true’,” by the addition of “when applied to (or denied of) propositions” is essential: because the English word “true” is ambiguous — sometimes used with a meaning that’s very far from the one that my minimalism and Künne’s definition are aiming to capture. For example, one might say, “She’s a true friend” and “He was a true hero”, where what’s meant (roughly speaking) is “She’s a paradigmatic, or perfect example of a friend” and “He was a paradigmatic, or perfect example of a hero”. But when we describe a certain proposition as true, we are never saying that it’s a perfect, or paradigmatic example of a proposition!!

In section 3, I’ll respond to a valuably challenging essay by the late Sten Lindström, in which he maintains that my Minimalism is substantially inadequate, because of the many truth-claims that it cannot accommodate (or so he argues — but I disagree!)

After that — in the relatively short 4th section of the paper — I turn to the question of whether either of the proposals we have been considering — mine or Künne’s — can accommodate the plausible view that a statement that’s untranslatable into our language might nonetheless be true.

In Section 5 I describe and address a paradox that arises from applying the above-mentioned Equivalence Schema — “The proposition that p is true ↔ p”, to a person’s assertion that what s/he is asserting is not true.

And finally — in light of those discussions — I’ll offer (in section 6) my answers to some provocative questions posed in the announcement of the University of Vienna’s last-September conference on TRUTH:

  1. Is truth definable?
  2. Is propositional quantification coherent?
  3. Do natural languages involve propositional quantification, and in what sense?
  4. What do the answers to these questions mean for philosophical attempts to define or explain truth?

SECTION 1 previous trials

Each of the proposed traditional accounts of truth took the form of a definition that purported to articulate (i) the meaning of the English word “true” (when it’s predicated of propositions), and (ii) the underlying nature of the property of being true.

Here are some prominent examples of such proposed definitions — each one followed by the grounds for rejecting it:

1. “x is true ↔ x corresponds to a fact”

But: the concept of “correspondence” is itself mysterious, yet unexplained. And — even worse — it’s arguable that the term of itself — the word “fact”, should be defined as “true proposition”! — In which case 1. defines “true” in terms of itself — which is absurd!!

Tim Maudlin tells me that deflationism is just another kind of correspondence theory. We can revisit this argument later. To see more about correspondence theory, go check [https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/truth/#CorRev].

The correspondence theory of truth expresses the very natural idea that truth is a content-to-world or word-to-world relation: what we say or think is true or false in virtue of the way the world turns out to be.

Correspondence theory is a very natural way of thinking of the truth as a content-to-world relation.

2. “x is true ↔ the possibility articulated by x is actualized”

But surely, a possibility is simply a proposition (one that could be true). And calling a proposition “actualized” is just an awkward way of saying that it’s true!!

3. Putnam “x is true ↔ a rational investigation would dictate believing x.”

There Putnam glosses truth as what would be justified under ideal epistemic conditions. With the pragmatists, Putnam sees the ideal conditions as something which can be approximated, echoing the idea of truth as the end of inquiry. (SEP)

But many truths are inaccessible to our investigations — either too remote, or too deep, or too complex, or too conceptually-alien. And rational considerations often point away from the truth! Moreover: rational considerations rarely deliver certainty. And being 90% confident in the truth of 100 independent hypotheses implies that their conjunction is probably false!

4. “x is true ↔ x is almost universally believed”

But: surely everyone could agree on a given point, yet all be mistaken. Consensus can change over time. And when people are divided, one of them must be right — but the true one is not almost universally believed.

5. Peirce “x is true ↔ x will be believed in the limit of enquiry.”

But: disagreements may never be ironed out. Agreement in the very distant future cannot ground truth-claims now. And even permanent agreement cannot guarantee correctness.

6. William James “x is true ↔ decisions based on assuming x will promote practical success.”

But: actions based on a person’s true beliefs can (unluckily) have catastrophic consequences. Conversely, false beliefs can (luckily) have life-saving consequences. James’ idea captures something about the value of true beliefs, but cannot define truth.

5. Foucault “x is true ↔ x is what the political powers pressure us to believe.”

But: political powers often pressure us to believe what is false. And many matters are ones the regime does not care about. Power may determine what is taken to be true, but not what is true.

SECTION 2 Künne’s schema

Regarding the two deflationary accounts of truth — mine and Künne’s — I’m confident that almost everyone here is prepared to bet that I will proceed by standing up for my own view, and by criticizing Professor Künne’s view.

But those people would lose their money!! For I don’t share the common complaint about Künne’s account:

  1. that quantification into sentence positions is unintelligible.
  2. or the implied view that classical logic, with quantifiers
    • “(∀x)(Fx)” for “everything is F”
    • “(∃x)(Fx)” for “something is F” there are no sentences of ordinary language, whose logical forms are best captured by quantifying into sentence positions.

On the contrary, quantifying into declarative sentence positions is easy to understand and quite useful. The term “declarative” is inserted here to make it clear that Künne does not endorse quantification into the position of sentences that are questions like “is the window open?” or imperatives like “open the window!” but only into the positions of sentences that express propositions like “the window is open.”

It is easy to understand quantification in positions of declarative sentences. Consider the excluded middle rule (∀p)(p∨∼p), and to be disposed to accept the schema is to be disposed to accept any instance of the schema p∨∼p. Similarly, for the case of truth, we shall recognize that a person’s disposition to accept instances of the equivalence schema ┌p┐ is true⇔p amounts to his/her being disposed to accept (∀p)(┌p┐ is true⇔p)

As for Künne’s proposed definition of “true:

(∀x)(x is true⇔(∃p)(x=┌p┐∧p))

I have just offered a defense of Künne’s quantifying with a universal quantifier, into sentence positions. But one might worry that this could not help us to understand his definition of truth, since that definition involves existential quantification into a sentence position.

It could be easily dispelled by propositional logic, and we can turn the statement (∃p)(x=┌p┐∧p) into

∼(∀p)(∼(x=┌p┐∧p))

So the disposition to accept Künne’s equivalence schema consists in the pair of dispositions. Given acceptance of ”x is true” and "x=┌p┐" to accept p. And given acceptance of some instance of the schema "x=┌p┐∧p" to accept ”x is true”.

Comments： I do not understand what he is talking about in the matter of logic. If we restricted the language to formal language like PA, it could be totally understandable, since we could stipulate the axioms as true propositions. However, here I think every reader would either think the equivalence schema is trivially right or that it says nothing because no instance of true propositions can be enumerated with the guidance of the schema. Horwich, in fact, I think he would respond like, saying that you cannot define truth, but it still has a meaning. Like you say, snow is white is true. For sure, it is true because snow is white. Once you have accepted that snow is white, you have to accept that it is true. We can check the following examples he gave. Since I didn’t think Horwich explained it clearly in class, I would show some of my interpretations of his theory later.

SECTION 3 objections

Sten Lindström suggested my approach cannot explain how we might arrive at truth-claims such as: (a) The Continuum Hypothesis is true. (b) There are true propositions that aren’t supported by available evidence. (c) Every sentence is such that either it or its negation is true. (d) Most statements that Clinton made in his deposition were true.

And I must concede that minimalism lacks the resources to rearticulate any of those truth-claims without using the word “true.”

But Künne’s definition, by contrast, can rearticulate (a) and (b) but not (c) and (d). But the minimalist has other resources: though we can’t define these claims without “true,” we can explain how we arrive at them.

For example, to reach (a):

We identify the Continuum Hypothesis, and we accept: “ is the Continuum Hypothesis ↔ p”. Thus by Leibniz’s Law we infer: “The Continuum Hypothesis is true.”

Comments: One may hold that he actually says nothing. The explanation was based on an extremely important premise, that is, one shall accept the continuum hypothesis. We have to acknowledge that Horwich’s intention was to give nothing epistemic, no commitment to maths, no commitment to science, no commitment metaphysically to the world. However, he would claim that you shall accept the equivalence schema, and truth is governed by instances of the schema.

meaning of truth relation_to_putnam

Similarly for (b):

Consider a proposition A which is neither supported nor unsupported by any evidence. Thus at least one propositions are true and unsupported by evidence, that is:

A∨∼A

Comments: sophistry

For (c):

Start by accepting many instances:

Either A or ∼A, Either B or ∼B, etc.

Generalize to: “Every declarative (= proposition-expressing) sentence is such that either it, or its negation, is true.”

Comments: this is more like a correspondence theory. What I was thinking was taking the language where there is an excluded middle rule, which can be related to Putnam and Carnap as well.

For (d):

SECTION 4 different languages

The issue here is how the two approaches under present consideration — mine and Künne’s — deal with the prospect of true statements articulated using sentences that aren’t translatable into our language. For simplicity, let’s imagine that we understand nothing but English.

Suppose F is a language, many of whose sentences are not translatable into English.

But suppose F contains an operator, “{Y}”, that, when applied to any categorical sentence of F — e.g. FS(k) — yields a singular term, “{Y}(FS(k))”, which, like an English that-clause, refers to the proposition expressed by FS(k).

In that case, it could be that language F contains a word — perhaps “fooo” — whose meaning is the same as the English “true”. For it could be that F-speakers are disposed to accept, for every p, instances of:

{FS(n)} is fooo⇔FS(n)

And, if so, then the way that F-speakers use their word fooo is the same as the way in which English speakers use “true” — which is our disposition to accept instances of “the proposition that p is true ⇔ p”.

Presumably, some of F’s untranslatable-into-English sentences might be true. But English speakers will not be able to tell whether or not this is so. For only if we could translate FS(k) could we formulate the instance of our equivalence schema that would enable us to specify the truth-condition of FS(k).

And Künne’s definition of “x is true” couldn’t solve this problem either; for it will be impossible for us to tell whether or not:

(∃p)({FS(k)}=┌p┐∧p)

Comments: Frankly, I acknowledge Horwich’s argument that it is impossible for us to tell whether or not FS(k) is true. The answer is trivial because it is not English (or any other language that we perceive). In Künne’s schema, if we take the "=" just as the identity, then p is in F and if there is a way for us to know whether p holds, we can say that FS(k) is fooo in F, and true (in English) according to the biconditional. I think the result quite conforms to our intuition. However, in Horwich’s schema, we have to take a step back and only commit to {FS(n)} is fooo⇔FS(n). In this sense, Künne’s truth holds across different languages, that is, he can assert the truth inside a language L with another language L′, while Horwich can only assert the truth inside the language L by L. If I am right, then Künne’s truth has a more general meaning (if we regard it as merely a meaning, not definition) than Horwich’s. (haven't been discussed)

SECTION 5 Liar paradox

(Horwich’s writing was really messy, and I am rewriting in a relatively more formal way.) Consider proposition p :

p⇔┌p┐ is false

Of course, one could refute that such a self-inference could be illegitimate, but we have to acknowledge that in ordinary languages (English), such self-inferences are inevitable (because the language is too strong). Not to mention, in formal systems, you can even devise a self-inference by diag (PA).

Then consider the equivalence schema, we will get ┌p┐ is true⇔p, which is

┌p┐ is true⇔┌p┐ is false

Now we infer that p is both true and not true.

Horwich thinks that at least one of our assumptions must be given up. It could be “every contradiction is false”, or the above p doesn’t express a proposition. Or alternatively, the assumption that every instance of the schema must be accepted.

It seems to me that this last alternative is the best way of escaping from the paradox. — For remember that minimalism’s meaning-theory demands no more than an inclination to accept instances of the equivalence schema. And in this case, the inclination is overwhelmed by the paradoxical consequences of accepting it.

Comments: I don’t like Horwich’s picture for two reasons. (Notice here I don’t mean he is not coherent, I just think it is not that minimalistic as he or I expected).

First, the disposal of the liar paradox indicates the redundancy in his theory. He didn’t explicitly introduce meta-language to defuse the liar paradox, but implicitly used an operational meta-language. He suggests that we should exclude some instances of the equivalence schema, where the operation “exclude” can only happen in a meta-language outside the object language where we were asserting truth. Then the problem arises if we contemplate the object language, for Horwich never said what the object language is. Is it PA? Is it SA? Or is it just ordinary language? If we add different language frameworks into the consideration (it is very natural if you are a minimalist like Horwich, because you want the truth to be perceived as easy as possible, so you can adjust your meta-language according to your object language), we might collapse into truth pluralism. Therefore, truth loses its status as a unified, stable, meaningful (this word is controversial though) concept.

Second, even though we fix the object language, merely denying that every instance conforms to the schema seems to cause more issues, for there are no clear instructions on which instance we shall give up the schema. One may say, the paradoxical propositions are countable since the words are finite in English (here and the discussion below can be related to an analogue of PA computability). However, Kripke has demonstrated that there is no algorithm to enumerate the paradoxical propositions recursively; that is, whether or not a sentence is paradoxical is not decidable. Then I would say the equivalence schema, as well as the operational meta-language, is flawed and leaky, since we don’t even know when the schema can not be applied.

SECTION 6 answer to 5 questions

Here are my answers to the 5 questions posed in the announcement of Vienna’s September 2024 conference on TRUTH.

Is truth definable?

The word “truth” is the name of the monadic property that’s attributed to a proposition when we predicate “true” of it. And properties aren’t objects of definition.

So I suspect the intended question is: “Is the English word ‘true’ definable?”

What’s implied by an affirmative answer is that a good definition can be devised, where the potential disqualifying defects include:

  • reliance on a term (in the proposed defining expression) that’s no less in need of definition than “true” is, or
  • The proposed defining expression includes a term (e.g. “fact”) that’s pretty clearly defined in terms of “true.”

I think the best candidate for a good definition of “true” is Wolfgang Künne’s. But one might well feel that its quality depends on whether a clarifying definition of “proposition” can be provided. However, I must concede that the absence of any such clarification of “proposition” would count against my minimalist proposal no less than it counts against Wolfgang’s definition of “true”.

Remarks: Russel denied the existence of the proposition, but I am not sure whether it was in Horwich’s sense. Horwich said to me multiple times that he doesn’t want an (analytic) definition for truth, and he thinks pursuing definitions leads to infinite regress. Therefore, he thinks we shall choose truth as the concept that has a meaning without definition to end the regress. (And meaning is just use, in a Wittgenstein’s sense) What is most important here is that we have to understand that in this picture, there is no metaphysical commitment to truth; that is, the schema itself won’t give any more knowledge (or knowledge of knowledge) about the cognitive object. What else he would say may probably (given by GPT):

  • Truth is a logical tool, not a property of reality;
  • The equivalence schema is meaning-constitutive
  • You learn the meaning of “true” by learning a rule: Anytime you accept 𝑝, you are also inclined to accept “𝑝 is true”.

Is propositional quantification coherent?

Obviously, we can coherently quantify over propositions — e.g.: “There is a proposition expressed by the English sentence, ‘Snow is white’ ” Or in other words:

“(∃x)(x is a proposition ∧ x is expressed in English by ‘Snow is white’).”

So I assume that the intended question is: “Is it coherent to quantify into declarative-sentence positions?”

And to this very different question, my answer is also “YES.” For, as I suggested in Section 2, it’s plausible that our acceptance of:

(∃p)(#...p)

consists in our acceptance of the instance of (#...p).
